# Rome Never Fell
In 476, Rome fell. Except it didn't. From a Greek harbour town, Rome ruled for nearly a thousand more years. {NUMBER: 1,000 MORE YEARS} This is the Byzantine Empire: riots, plague, secret fire and betrayal by friends. {TITLE: THE BYZANTINE EMPIRE} So why was a Roman emperor still fighting in 1453?

# New Rome [card: 330 AD]
Our story starts in 330, when Constantine rebuilt Byzantion, on the Bosphorus, as "New Rome." {NAME: CONSTANTINE I} In 395, the empire split between two brothers, East and West. {MAP} Around 413, giant walls went up: 5.7 kilometres long, with 96 towers. {NUMBER: 5.7 KM · 96 TOWERS} In 476, a warlord kicked out the last western emperor | and posted his crown and robes to Constantinople. The western half collapsed. The Roman Empire did not. {SPLIT}

The walls could stop any army. But the danger was inside, at the racetrack.

# Riot, Dome and Plague [card: 532 AD]
Under Justinian, chariot-racing fans belonged to rival gangs: the Blues and the Greens. {NAME: JUSTINIAN} In 532, both gangs rioted, chanting "Nika!", and burned the city centre. Justinian packed to flee. According to the historian Procopius, Empress Theodora said she'd rather die in imperial purple than run. {NAME: THEODORA} So General Belisarius sealed the Hippodrome gates. {NAME: BELISARIUS} Procopius claims about 30,000 people died inside. {NUMBER: ~30,000}

Weeks later, work began on a new Hagia Sophia. It opened in 537, with a dome 31 metres wide. {NUMBER: 31 M DOME} For nearly a thousand years, no cathedral on Earth was bigger. Justinian also squeezed Roman law into one code. Then his generals retook North Africa and Italy. {MAP} But in 541, plague struck, probably carried by rats on grain ships.

The wars and plague drained Rome, | just as a new power rose from the desert.

# Greek Fire and Betrayal [card: 636 AD]
Arab armies took Syria in 636, then Egypt, the empire's breadbasket. {MAP} Twice, in 674 and 717, Arab forces besieged Constantinople. The Romans answered with Greek fire: liquid flame that burned on water. By 718, fire, frost and Bulgar raiders had shattered the attackers.

Later, Basil II, the Bulgar-Slayer, conquered Bulgaria. {NAME: BASIL II} A later chronicler claims he blinded 15,000 Bulgarian prisoners. The empire's gold coin, the solidus, was the dollar of the Middle Ages. {METAPHOR} But after Basil, the gold got watered down. In 1071, at Manzikert, the Turks captured an emperor. {MAP} In 1204, a crusade steered by Venice's blind old leader | looted the greatest Christian city. {NAME: ENRICO DANDOLO}

The Romans won it back 57 years later, | but a new enemy was waiting.

# The Last Constantine [card: 1453]
Then the final boss arrived: Sultan Mehmed II, aged 21. {NAME: MEHMED II} He brought giant cannons built by a gunsmith named Orban. He'd reportedly offered them to the Romans first, who couldn't pay. Stone balls smashed walls that had held for a thousand years. Mehmed dragged about 70 ships overland on greased logs, past the harbour chain. {MAP} About 7,000 defenders faced up to 80,000 attackers.

But here's the twist most people forget. The last emperor was Constantine, son of Helena. {NAME: CONSTANTINE XI} So was the city's first emperor, 1,123 years earlier. {SPLIT} On 29 May 1453, the walls broke. Constantine XI died fighting, and his body was never identified.

Rome was dead. Then someone renamed it.

# Never Byzantine [card: 1557]
Even Mehmed called himself "Caesar of Rome." But in 1557, a German scholar popularised a new label: Byzantine.

So why does this matter today? Fleeing Greek scholars carried ancient books to Italy, fuelling the Renaissance. Orthodox Christianity and the Cyrillic alphabet spread north from Byzantium. Russia adopted the double-headed eagle and called Moscow the "Third Rome." {MAP} Justinian's code still shapes civil law across Europe and Latin America.

Nobody who lived there ever called themselves Byzantine. To the last man on the walls, they were Romans.
