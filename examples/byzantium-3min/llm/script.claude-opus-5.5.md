# Rome Never Fell
In 476, Rome fell. Except it didn't. A Greek harbour town ruled as Rome for 1,123 years. {NUMBER: 1,123 YEARS} Its leftovers shape your laws, Russia's alphabet and mosque domes worldwide. This is the Byzantine Empire: riots, plague, secret fire and betrayal by friends. {TITLE: THE BYZANTINE EMPIRE} If Rome fell in 476, who was ruling it in 1453?

# New Rome [card: 11 May 330, Byzantion, the Bosphorus]
Our story starts in 330, when Emperor Constantine rebuilt Byzantion, on the Bosphorus, as "New Rome." {NAME: CONSTANTINE I} {MAP} In 395, the empire split between two brothers, East and West. {MAP} Around 413, triple walls went up, 5.7 kilometres long, with 96 towers. {NUMBER: 5.7 KM · 96 TOWERS} In 476, a warlord kicked out the last western emperor | and posted his crown and robes to Constantinople. The western half collapsed. The Roman Empire did not. {SPLIT}

The walls could stop any army. But the danger was inside, at the racetrack.

# Riot, Dome and Plague [card: January 532, the Hippodrome]
Under Justinian, chariot fans split into gangs: Blues and Greens. {NAME: JUSTINIAN} In 532, both gangs rioted, chanting "Nika!", or "Win!", and burned the city centre. Justinian packed to flee. According to the historian Procopius, Empress Theodora said she'd rather die in imperial purple than run. {NAME: THEODORA} So General Belisarius sealed the Hippodrome gates. {NAME: BELISARIUS} Procopius claims about 30,000 people died inside. {NUMBER: ~30,000 (ACCORDING TO PROCOPIUS)}

Five weeks later, work began on a new Hagia Sophia. {SPLIT} It opened in 537, with a dome 31 metres wide. {NUMBER: 31 M DOME} For a thousand years, no cathedral on Earth was bigger. Justinian also squeezed Roman law into one code. Then Belisarius retook North Africa and Italy. {MAP} But in 541, plague broke out, carried by rats on grain ships. Historians still argue how deadly it was.

The wars and plague drained Rome, | just as a new power rose from the desert.

# Greek Fire and Betrayal [card: 636, the Yarmouk River]
Arab armies took Syria in 636, then Egypt, the empire's breadbasket. {MAP} From 674 to 678, and again in 717, Arab forces pressed Constantinople. The Romans answered with Greek fire: liquid flame that burned on water. By 718, fire, frost and Bulgar raiders had shattered the attackers.

Later, Basil II, the Bulgar-Slayer, ruled for 49 years and conquered Bulgaria. {NAME: BASIL II} {NUMBER: 49 YEARS} A later chronicler claims he blinded 15,000 Bulgarian prisoners. His gold solidus was the dollar of the Middle Ages. {METAPHOR} But after him, the gold got watered down. {SPLIT} In 1071, at Manzikert, the Turks captured an emperor. {MAP} In 1204, a crusade steered by Venice's blind old leader | looted the greatest Christian city. {NAME: ENRICO DANDOLO}

The Romans won it back 57 years later, | but a new enemy was waiting.

# The Last Constantine [card: 6 April 1453, the land walls]
Then the final boss arrived: Sultan Mehmed II, aged 21. {NAME: MEHMED II} {NUMBER: AGE 21} He brought giant cannons built by a gunsmith named Orban. {NAME: ORBAN} Orban had reportedly offered them to the Romans first, but they couldn't pay. Stone balls smashed walls that had held for a thousand years. Mehmed dragged about 70 ships overland on greased logs, past the harbour chain. {MAP} {NUMBER: ~70 SHIPS} About 7,000 defenders faced up to 80,000 attackers. {NUMBER: 7,000 VS 80,000}

But here's the twist most people forget. The last emperor was Constantine, son of Helena. {NAME: CONSTANTINE XI} So was the first, 1,123 years earlier. {SPLIT} On 29 May 1453, the walls broke. Constantine XI died in the assault; his body was never certainly identified.

Rome was dead. Then, a century later, someone renamed it.

# Never Byzantine [card: 1557, a printing house in Germany]
Even Mehmed called himself "Caesar of Rome." But in 1557, a German scholar, Hieronymus Wolf, popularised a new label: Byzantine.

So why does any of this matter today? Fleeing Greek scholars carried ancient books to Italy, fuelling the Renaissance. Orthodox Christianity and the Cyrillic alphabet spread north from Byzantium. Russia adopted the double-headed eagle and called Moscow the "Third Rome." {MAP} Justinian's code still shapes civil law across Europe and Latin America.

Nobody who lived there ever called themselves Byzantine. To the last man on the walls, they were Romans.
