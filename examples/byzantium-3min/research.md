# Fact Sheet: The Byzantine Empire

## 1. One-line arc

A Roman emperor renames a Greek harbour town on the Bosphorus "New Rome" in AD 330. That city outlives Rome itself by nearly a thousand years. In 1453 it falls to 21-year-old Sultan Mehmed II's cannons, and its last emperor is a Constantine, son of a Helena, just like its first.

## 2. The central paradox

**How did the "fallen" Roman Empire keep running for another thousand years, and why don't most people know it existed?**

Alternate phrasing: *If Rome fell in 476, who was ruling the Roman Empire in 1453?*

## 6. Legends vs evidence

- **"Rome fell in 476."** This is a convention rather than an event that contemporaries treated as the end of Rome. The East kept the Roman state, law, taxes and title. Say: *"The western half collapsed. The Roman Empire did not."*
- **Theodora's purple-shroud speech.** This is reported only by Procopius (*Wars* 1.24), who was writing history in the classical style, where invented speeches were normal. The punchline, that royal purple makes a fine burial shroud, adapts an older Greek saying about tyranny. **Do not quote it as verbatim. Paraphrase it:** *"According to the historian Procopius, Theodora told the council she would rather die in imperial purple than run."* Note too that Procopius' *Secret History* is a hostile, gossip-filled attack on her, so he is not a neutral source.
- **Nika death toll of 30,000.** This is an ancient figure. Hedge it: *"Procopius claims about 30,000."*
- **"Solomon, I have surpassed thee!"** Justinian supposedly said this at Hagia Sophia's opening, but it comes from a much later, legend-filled text (the *Narratio*, ~9th c.). **Legend.**
- **Plague death toll.** "Half of Europe died" is not established. Recent studies (e.g. Mordechai & Eisenberg, 2019) argue the impact was smaller and uneven. Say: *"Historians still argue how deadly it was."* Procopius' "10,000 a day" is a peak claim and should be hedged.
- **Greek fire.** The recipe is unknown, likely petroleum-based and sprayed through pressurised siphons. "It burned on water" is broadly reported. Kallinikos as inventor rests on Theophanes, writing ~140 years later.
- **The 674–678 "siege."** Some scholars (Jankowiak, 2013) argue the five-year siege is a later construction and it was really a blockade or series of raids. Say: *"Arab fleets pressed the city for years."*
- **Basil blinding 15,000.** The figure comes from Skylitzes, writing decades later. The claim that Tsar Samuel died of shock at the sight is dramatic and may be embellished. The nickname "Bulgar-Slayer" became popular mainly after Basil's death (see Stephenson, *The Legend of Basil the Bulgar-Slayer*).
- **Manzikert as the instant loss of Anatolia.** The defeat was real, but the Byzantine civil wars that followed did as much damage. Alp Arslan released Romanos within about a week, on treaty terms.
- **The Kerkoporta.** The story that a small gate left unlocked let the Ottomans in during 1453 comes from Doukas alone. **Disputed.**
- **Constantine XI's death.** Accounts conflict: he died fighting, his body was identified by its eagle-embroidered boots, or it was never found. The image of him throwing off his regalia is **legend-tinged**. Safest line: *"He died in the final assault; his body was never certainly identified."*
- **Hieronymus Wolf "invented" the term.** He did not coin "Byzantine" from nothing, but his 1557 collection popularised it as a label for the empire. Writers such as Du Cange, Montesquieu and Gibbon later spread it. Say: *"popularised"* or *"a label scholars applied after the fall."*
- **Cyril invented Cyrillic.** Cyril and Methodius created **Glagolitic**. Cyrillic was developed by their disciples in Bulgaria (~late 9th c.) and named in Cyril's honour.
- **Smuggled silkworms.** The story is reported by Procopius (monks carried eggs in hollow canes, according to later writers). It is plausible but **hedge**.
- **The fork reaching Europe via a Byzantine princess.** A Byzantine bride in Venice (~1004) was reportedly mocked by churchmen for eating with a golden fork. **Hedge as anecdote.**

## 7. Twists and ironies (best first)

1. **The first and last emperor were both Constantine, son of Helena.** Constantine I's mother was Helena, and Constantine XI's mother was Helena Dragaš, a Serbian princess. Use this as the re-hook at ~75%.
2. **No "Byzantine" ever called himself Byzantine.** They called themselves *Rhomaioi*, meaning Romans. Even their enemies agreed: the Turks called the land *Rum* ("Rome"), and Mehmed II took the title *Kayser-i Rum*, "Caesar of Rome."
3. **The worst sack came from Christians, not Muslims.** In 1204, crusaders who had set out to fight in the Holy Land looted Constantinople instead. The four bronze horses on St Mark's Basilica in Venice (originals now inside) are part of that loot.
4. **The cannon-maker came to the Byzantines first.** Orban reportedly offered his skills to Constantine XI, who could not pay him enough, so he went to Mehmed **(verify; source: Doukas)**.
5. **A riot over chariot racing nearly ended the empire's greatest reign,** and led directly to one of the world's most famous buildings.
6. **Justinian's dream of restoring Rome may have weakened it.** His wars plus the plague drained the empire just before the Arab conquests **(historians' interpretation)**.
7. **The walls were never stormed until gunpowder.** In 1204 the crusaders broke in from the harbour side; the land walls held for about 1,000 years until 1453.
8. **Europe's legal systems rest on a book that was nearly lost.** Justinian's Digest was largely forgotten in the West until a manuscript resurfaced in Italy and was studied at Bologna (~1100).
9. **Hagia Sophia has changed roles repeatedly:** church (537), Catholic cathedral under the crusaders (1204), mosque (1453), museum (1934), mosque again (2020).

## 8. Why it still matters

1. **Your country's law, possibly.** Civil-law systems across Europe, Latin America, Quebec and Louisiana descend from Roman law via Justinian's code. Examples include the Napoleonic Code (1804) and the German Civil Code (1900).
2. **The Cyrillic alphabet,** used today by roughly 250 million people **(verify)** in Russia, Ukraine, Bulgaria, Serbia and elsewhere.
3. **Orthodox Christianity,** roughly 200–260 million followers **(verify)**, including the icons and onion-domed churches descended from Byzantine models.
4. **Russia's double-headed eagle** on its coat of arms, adopted by Ivan III's Moscow after his 1472 Palaiologan marriage. The eagle is also on the flags of Albania, Serbia and Montenegro.
5. **"Third Rome":** the idea, voiced by the monk Philotheus of Pskov (~1510s–1520s), that Moscow was Rome's heir. "Tsar" itself comes from "Caesar."
6. **The Renaissance.** Manuel Chrysoloras taught Greek in Florence from 1397. Cardinal Bessarion gave his library of ~750 manuscripts (most of them Greek) to Venice in 1468, founding the Biblioteca Marciana **(verify count)**. Much of Plato and Greek science survives through Byzantine copies.
7. **The word "byzantine"** meaning hopelessly complicated, an unfair modern stereotype.
8. **Istanbul's skyline:** Hagia Sophia, the land walls (a UNESCO site) and the Basilica Cistern still stand.
9. **Fifteen hundred years of Hagia Sophia's architecture:** its dome-on-pendentives design shaped Ottoman mosques, which shaped mosques worldwide.
10. **The fork at the dinner table** (anecdotal; hedge).

## 10. Sources to verify

**Primary sources (read with caution):**
- Procopius: *Wars* (Nika, Theodora's speech, plague, reconquest), *Buildings* (Hagia Sophia) and *Secret History*, which is hostile.
- Theophanes the Confessor, *Chronographia*: Greek fire, the Arab sieges.
- John Skylitzes: Basil II and Kleidion.
- Michael Psellos, *Chronographia*: Basil's treasury and the debasement era.
- Niketas Choniates and Geoffrey of Villehardouin: 1204.
- Sphrantzes, Doukas, Kritoboulos and Nicolò Barbaro's diary: 1453 (troop counts, ships, Orban, Kerkoporta).

**Reference works:**
- *Oxford Dictionary of Byzantium* (ed. A. Kazhdan, 1991): the best single fact-check for dates and names.
- *Cambridge History of the Byzantine Empire* (ed. J. Shepard, 2008).
- Warren Treadgold, *A History of the Byzantine State and Society*: population and army figures.
- Anthony Kaldellis, *Romanland* (2019) and *The New Roman Empire* (2023): on Roman identity and the "Byzantine" label.

**Specialist works for the shakiest claims:**
- Marek Jankowiak, "The First Arab Siege of Constantinople" (2013): 674–678.
- Paul Stephenson, *The Legend of Basil the Bulgar-Slayer* (2003).
- Lee Mordechai and Merle Eisenberg et al., *PNAS* 2019; Wagner et al., *Lancet Infectious Diseases* 2014: plague impact and DNA.
- John Haldon on Greek fire.
- Philip Grierson, Dumbarton Oaks coin catalogues: solidus weight and purity, carat figures.
- Steven Runciman, *The Fall of Constantinople 1453*; Roger Crowley, *Constantinople: The Last Great Siege*: siege numbers, ships, cannon dimensions (cross-check against the Royal Armouries for the Dardanelles Gun).
- Rowland Mainstone, *Hagia Sophia*: dome dimensions.
- UNESCO and Ethnologue-type sources: Cyrillic user counts. Pew Research: Orthodox population.
- Marciana Library: Bessarion's manuscript count.
